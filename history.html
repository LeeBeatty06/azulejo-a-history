History: The Archives

Brief History

Origins

The story of the azulejo reflects centuries of cultural exchanges across the Iberian Peninsula. The word azulejo comes from the Arabic term al-zulayj, meaning a small, polished stone. Early tile traditions incorporated Islamic and Mudéjar techniques and patterns, while later Italian majolica, Dutch, and Flemish artistic influences expanded the possibilities for painted ceramic decoration. The traditional glazed ceramic tiles are made using multiple steps. First, they are shaped with red clay, molded into squares, and dried for weeks. They are then fired, glazed with a tin-oxide base and an opaque white layer, hand-painted with raw metal oxide pigments, and fired once more for their final finish.

Renaissance and European Influence

With the arrival of the Renaissance in Portugal during the sixteenth century, new painting techniques let tilemakers go beyond repeated pattern designs. Italian and Flemish influences through trade would bring different techniques of showing pictures and decorations such as classical architectural details and vegetal designs. This would make Portuguese tiled walls become increasingly complex. These styles would then be integrated into the surfaces of churches, palaces, and other buildings and environments.

Blue and White

Blue and white tilework became one of the most predominant and recognizable types of Portuguese azulejos. Portugal's sea trade connections brought Chinese porcelain into Lisbon during the Age of Discovery. European ceramic producers would adapt to the visual appeal of the blue decoration on pale surfaces in their own ceramics. Cobalt blue is ideal for glazing and ensured durability as well as visual appeal.
